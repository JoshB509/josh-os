## Hook

You can have the best idea in the company, and executives still just don't seem to get it, and the harder you explain it, the worse it gets.

After teaching more than 80,000 professionals how to get their ideas approved by senior people, I can tell you why. They never wanted more information. They're running a silent test, and unless your idea passes it, it dies, however good it was.

So in this video I'll show you what that test is, and the two things to bring with your idea that get executives to finally understand it and approve it, no matter how technical your work is.

## Question 1

The first box is the one people get wrong most often, and when I tell you what goes in it, you're probably going to think, “Well, I already do that.” Stay with me anyway, because this is the hardest box to fill properly, and it's hard because it feels like you've been filling it the whole time, which is exactly why it's so often the one left empty.

So the first thing an executive needs before they'll approve any idea is the answer to, “Does this have any benefit?”

And if you're anything like the clients I work with, the reason this box stays empty is that you're describing outputs, when the executive is listening for outcomes, and that one difference is why the box never gets filled.

Because when an executive asks for more detail about your idea, the question underneath it is, “What does the business actually get from this?”

So when you say it increases processing speed by fifteen per cent, or it automates our marketing campaigns, or it forecasts our sales, those are features of the idea. They tell the executive what it does, and leave the box exactly as empty as it was.

Now you might be thinking, “Surely an executive knows that faster processing is a good thing.” Well, they do. That was never the problem. Knowing something's good still doesn't tell them what they'd get, so they've got nothing to picture and nothing to say yes to.

Think about buying a new phone. The new one has a 7,500 mAh battery, and the one in your pocket has 5,000. You know bigger is better, right? But does that tell you what you'd actually do with it? Not really.

Now say I told you that with that battery and that big screen, you could watch films the whole way through a long-haul flight and still get off the plane with charge to spare, without hunting for a socket at the airport or carrying a bulky power bank around in your bag.

Now you can picture it, and nothing about the battery changed. A bigger battery is a feature, and what fills the box is the benefit.

So let me show you a simple way to turn any feature into a benefit every executive wants, even if your work is too technical to fit in a sentence. Take the slide we started with. [ON SCREEN: the original slide]

The easiest thing to change is the title. “A new request-routing system” tells them what you've built and leaves them to work out whether they want it. So take the feature and keep asking “which means?” until you hit something the business gets. A routing system, which means requests go straight to the right team, which means customers hear back faster, which means this.

“We want to get customer replies down from three days to one, using the team we've already got.”

Then do the same to the bullets. “Integrates with our existing inbox”, which means the team doesn't have to learn a new tool, becomes “Live in weeks, with no retraining and no disruption to the team.” And “Real-time dashboard of open requests”, which means managers can see a request getting stuck, becomes “Stuck requests get spotted on day one, before the customer has to complain.” [ON SCREEN: the rewritten slide]

And when you open with that? They're picturing the result before you've shown them a single routing rule, and every slide after it becomes the answer to a question they're now asking.

So that's the first box filled, and they've got a reason to keep listening.

But that still leaves you with a problem, because wanting something doesn't mean they'll agree to it, and the second box is still empty, and what you put in the second box is what's going to signal to seanior leaders that you're operating at their level because they're going to finally be able to agree to your ideas.

## Question 2

Right, so say I want a Bentley, and I want one badly. I can't afford one, but I want one. You could show me the seats, the engine, every colour it comes in, and I'd probably want it even more.

But am I going to the dealership this afternoon? No, because wanting it is just the first box.

And the executive can be doing exactly that with your idea. “Replies in one day? I'd love that. But have you seen how busy that team is?”

So you explain the benefits again, and they agree with every one of them. You walk out wondering why they won't let you start, while they're wondering how you think that team could possibly deliver it, and because you answered with more benefits, it starts to look like you haven't thought about how the business actually runs.

So the second thing is. “Do I believe we can actually do this?”

And most professionals answer that with a detailed “Rollout plan”, but no matter how well planned your implementation is, it doesn't provide the box with an answer, because they box was never looking for the plan, it just wanted to know if the plan is easy to do.

And the simple change I'm about to show you does exactly that.

[ON SCREEN: the original slide] See all this stuff here about the plan? Delete it. Now, that's probably going to worry you, because you're thinking, “But all that detail is the plan. How will they understand it without it?” Don't worry, because that detail was making your idea less appealing. It gave them less confidence in it, and let me show you why.

Say I told you how to make a hot dog like this. Take one pre-cooked frankfurter and place it in a microwave-safe vessel. Apply eight hundred watts of microwave energy at a frequency of 2.45 gigahertz for thirty seconds, so the water molecules inside it oscillate and raise its core temperature above seventy-four degrees Celsius. Then transfer it to a pre-split bun and apply your condiments in a controlled zig-zag pattern along its length.

Sounds hard, right? I'm confused, and it's a hot dog.

But if I said, “Pop it in the microwave for thirty seconds and enjoy,” you'd think, easy, I can do that.

And that's what you need to do on your slide. But before you change anything, you need to know there's a difference between a vague plan and an easy plan. A vague plan worries them, because it looks like you don't actually know what you're doing. An easy plan is what they want, because they can see every step and none of them looks hard.

So that's why on the slide, I'm putting this. [ON SCREEN: the technical rollout rewritten as three steps that deliver the result. “1. Turn on routing for one team's inbox. 2. And when they see that? The question about how busy the team is answers itself, because they can see exactly how this team gets replies down to one day, and the second box is filled.
