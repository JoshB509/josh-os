# Written guides

There are three kinds of written document. Know which one you're making before you write a word.

| Kind | When | Organised by | Made by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lesson guide** | One per lesson, beside the video in the course app | The lesson's script, in order | The lesson guide job (`10_lessons_guides-thumbnails-upload.md`). Never changed by this skill. |
| **Lesson companion** | Josh wants the ideas of a set of lessons in one document to read and keep | The ideas, roughly in lesson order | A written guide job, kind `companion` |
| **Working document** | The reader has already studied the lessons and is now sitting in front of the thing they have to write (a goals form, a review, an update, a business case) | The writing task, step by step, never lesson order | A written guide job, kind `working` |

If Josh says the reader "has done the lessons", "is filling in the form", "needs to write it", or asks for something "tactical", "practical" or "to use", it's a working document. If he says "a companion to the lessons" or "a guide to the lessons", it's a companion. If it isn't clear, make a working document, and say in your reply which you made.

## Both kinds

- House style is the lesson guide kit in `Heard_at_the_Top\Lesson_Guides\_source`. Read `BRIEF.md`, the exemplar `lessons\3.1.html`, `base.css`, `extra.css` and the macro list at the top of `make.py`. Build with `studio/written_guide.py`, which never overwrites a version.
- The cover eyebrow is "CareerOS · Guide" for a companion and "CareerOS · Working document" for a working document.
- Josh's writing rules apply in full: no em or en dashes, no colons in prose, no "not X, but Y", none of the banned words, no fragment lists, British spelling, grade 12, second person, "I" only for Josh.
- Source material is Josh's real lessons. Say in your reply exactly which files you used. Never invent client results, never write "every client I've worked with" or similar, and never promise a rating, a promotion or an outcome. Write "gives your manager what they need to argue for it", never "gets you a five".
- Examples you write yourself are fine and needed, but say in your reply which ones are yours so Josh can check them.
- Check every page in the build's print picture, for layout (overflow, split figures, orphaned headings, big gaps) and for use (could someone with the form open actually use this page?). Fix and rebuild until both pass.

## Lesson companion

The structure in the house brief, adapted from a lesson guide: a cover with one row per section, an opening page with the reader's problem and an "In this guide" box, numbered sections following the ideas, figures with notes, one pull quote, a short close. Four to eight pages.

## Working document

The reader already knows why. They're sitting with the form open and a blinking cursor. Every page should help them write the next line. The practical style reference is the BENS Dictionary (`Heard_at_the_Top\Bonuses\BENS-Dictionary\2026-10_bens-dictionary_swipe-file_v02.html`). Read its opening and one letter's section before you start, and match how it works: the shape of a sentence shown as a strip of parts, weak and strong versions side by side with a line on why, word lists to build from, and lots of finished lines to lift.

**Structure, in the order of the writing task:**

1. **Cover.** The task as a promise ("Write goals and a review your manager can argue for"), and one row per step of the task.
2. **The short version.** Half a page to a page recapping the reasoning from the lessons, as the few rules everything after rests on. No story, no lesson retelling.
3. **One section per step of the task,** in the order they'll do it (for a review form that's gathering evidence, then writing each goal, then the summary, then the conversation). Each step has:
   - **The shape.** The structure of a good line or paragraph, as a strip of parts the reader fills in.
   - **Before and after.** Many varied rewrites, across different functions and levels (finance, HR, operations, sales, technology, projects, people managers and individual contributors), each with the trap the before falls into and one line on why the after works. Aim for eight to fifteen per main step, never one or two.
   - **Phrases to copy.** Sentence starters and connectors grouped by what they do, ready to paste.
   - **Questions to get it out of your head.** Prompts that help the reader remember evidence and detail they've forgotten (what changed, who noticed, what would have happened, what it replaced).
   - **Fill it in.** A worked fill-in box or table with blank lines the reader completes on the printed page, using the step's shape.
4. **Worked examples.** At least two full worked pieces end to end (for a review, two goals written up completely) so the reader sees the whole thing put together.
5. **The check.** A checklist to run before they submit, one line per trap.

Organise around the task, never around lesson numbers. Name the lessons once, on the short version page, as where to go for the why. Expect eight to fourteen pages, because the value is in the volume of usable examples and phrases.
