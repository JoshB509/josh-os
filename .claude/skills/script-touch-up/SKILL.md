# Script touch-up

## The one goal

Every script this skill touches should read like an Ed Lawrence script, about our audience's pains and desires. That is the whole job. Everything else in this file serves it, and nothing in this file overrides it except the house style list, which applies to every line. If a rule ever seems to be pushing a line towards something flatter, more repetitive or more formulaic than Ed would write, the goal wins and the line gets rewritten.

Here's what an Ed script is like. Go line by line through twelve of his scripts and around 85 per cent of lines are doing something to keep the viewer moving. The teaching is small, a chart, a rule, three steps, boiled down so it could be repeated after one hearing, and nearly everything wrapped around it is movement. He tells you where you are, why the next bit matters to you, and what you'll get if you stay. He complicates a point just as it settles ("The thing is…"), asks the question you're thinking and answers it in two words ("Would I buy that? Well, no."), says your doubt before you can, parks a promise and pays it off later, climbs the stakes a rung at a time, keeps one example running through the whole video, and ends on the next problem you've now got. It sounds like a person talking to you across a table, with every line in a different shape from the one before it.

Everything is an Ed move. Every signpost, every grounding line, every re-hook inside a section and every close is one of the moves in Part B, or a Parker line. If you can't name which move a line is, it isn't doing the job, so rewrite it as one.

The bank at the bottom of this file is the fuel. Part A has every core frustration of our audience said many different ways, with the results said just as many ways. Part B has Ed's moves, his original lines and ours. Part C has the vague words to swap out. Write from the bank. Lift lines from it, adapt them, or use them as the pattern for new ones in the same spirit.

## The audience

People who are good at their job and aren't getting the credit, the decisions or the promotions that should come with it. They know more than anyone in the meeting and it never comes across. Part A of the bank covers their communication frustrations (A1 to A15) and their career frustrations (K1 to K10). For the communication course (Heard at the Top), `08_Strategy/audience-communication.md` has their own words from sales calls.

Write to the core frustration so it fits everyone who has it, rather than repeating one person's quote. Weave more senior and less senior scenes into the same sentences, leaning one way then the other, rather than addressing two groups.

**The viewer is always the one on the line.** The viewer is the person whose idea, work or career is at stake, so every pain and every result is theirs. In a video about recommendations, ideas or proposals, they're the one with the good idea that keeps getting rejected, and the core pain is A15. They know it would help the business, the executive doesn't get it, it gets sent back for more detail until it dies, and they're the one who ends up labelled as someone who doesn't understand the business. Never reframe a lesson as coaching them to get someone else, like their boss, to carry it for them, unless the brief says so. The result is their idea approved, their name on it, and senior leaders asking for their opinion because they now see them as leadership material.

**Who they're talking to.** The people the viewer pitches to and reports up to are executives, senior leaders or senior decision makers. Use those words. Never make "the director" the stand-in. Specific roles (the CFO, the head of HR, your boss) are fine where the scene needs one.

## How to write it, in three passes

When Josh asks for a lesson to be written or rewritten, build it from the brief (what the lesson teaches, in what order) with a fresh skeleton and fresh examples. Editing the old draft line by line only gives an adjusted version of it, so do that only when he asks for a touch-up of specific lines.

**Before pass one, check the lead magnet.** Whenever you're writing or rewriting a whole script, ask Josh whether this video is promoting a lead magnet before you write anything. If it is, ask him which one, what it does for the viewer, and what's inside it. Never guess the contents or fill them in from a magnet's name. `05_Lead_Magnets/magnet-index.md` lists the magnets he has, so you can offer those as options when you ask, and each magnet's folder has a `magnet.md` and landing page copy that say what's inside. If he says there isn't one, the script has no lead magnet mentions at all. Touch-ups of a single paragraph or section don't need the question.

**Pass one, the skeleton.** Write the hook in its three beats (see The hook below), each section's idea boiled down to one or two sentences with one example or scene from their week, and the close. Decide whether there's a story and which of its two places it goes in (see Stories below), and if there's a lead magnet, mark the three points it'll be mentioned at, the first once the viewer has had the first real piece of value (never straight after the hook), then around the middle and around 80 per cent of the way through. Mark the places the video turns from the idea to the tangible thing (the slide, the line, the steps), because each of those gets a Parker line (see Parker lines below). Keep the teaching short. If a section's idea takes more than a paragraph to state, it's repeating itself, so find the one sentence that says it and let the example carry the rest.

**Pass two, the movement pass.** Go section by section and build the movement around the skeleton, drawing on the bank. Each section ends up running roughly like this.

1. A signpost into it (one of B1 to B12, B32, or a Parker line where the section shows the tangible thing), in a different shape from the last signpost, promising what they'll get from the section.
2. A grounding line that names what we're inside (B13).
3. The idea.
4. Then, every two to four sentences, a move from B15 to B30. A "the thing is", a "but worse", a quick question with a short answer, their doubt said out loud, a conditional that keeps someone in, a stakes rung, a callback, a mock voice, a result in their world. Mix them. Never use the same one twice in a row.
5. A line that closes the section (B14), or hands them the next problem so the next section arrives as the answer (B3).

Across the whole script, keep one example, image or number running through the whole thing and call back to it (B26). When the script has a story, the story is usually that running example, and the rest of the script keeps pointing back to it. Park an open loop only where there's a genuine gap the viewer wants closed, and pay it off by giving them the thing (B21). Drop in two or three client signals along the way, in signposts or keep-watching lines (see Client signals below).

**Pass three, the joins.** Read the whole script top to bottom as one conversation, with each signpost next to the paragraphs either side of it. Wherever a paragraph starts cold, or a signpost would make the viewer ask "what part?" or "what four things?", add the join. The moves are what make a script feel like Ed, and the joins are what make it feel like one person talking, so a script needs both.

## The rules that shape every line

These are all the rules there are. They don't compete with each other.

**Every signpost is a named Ed move, and it promises what's coming.** Before you write a signpost, decide which move it is, from B1 to B12, B32 or a Parker line, and write it in that move's shape. If you can't name the move, the line doesn't go in. A signpost has to make the viewer want what's next, so it carries a promise of what they'll get from it (a result in their world) or a curiosity gap they want closed. A pain on its own isn't enough. This applies to the very first line after the hook as much as anywhere.

Three kinds of line keep slipping through and are banned as signposts.

- A question that only asks where to start or what to do ("So where do you start when you've got an idea you know would help the business, and every time you take it to the executive you're sent away for more detail?"). It has the pain in it and promises nothing, so it's neutral.
- A line that moves into an example or a walkthrough ("So let's take one idea and follow it all the way through"). It's a signpost too, and it gives them no reason to care about the example.
- A line that promises to explain where, why or how something goes wrong ("So let's start with what happens after you've explained it, because that's where most recommendations stall"). It makes the insight the prize.

The fix is a move that promises the result. Client proof with a twist (B32) does it best at the start of a lesson, a line that removes the pain they came in with (B9) or leads with the result (B8) does it anywhere, and a Parker line does it at every turn into the mechanics.

**Every paragraph picks up from the one before it.** The viewer should always know why we've moved on to this paragraph. Open it with the link, the way you would in speech. "So let me show you what that looks like." "Now put that next to a budget request." "Now let's go back to that meeting from the start." "Which means the next time you hear one of those…" "So if they weren't preparing more than me, what were they actually doing?" A paragraph that starts cold, like "Go back to the showroom." straight after a definition, leaves the viewer wondering why we're talking about this.

**Introduce it, then name it, then say what it is, then show it.** Tell them something is coming before you name it. "So the system that turns your forty slides into two sentences and a decision has four parts to it, and it's called BENS. BENS is the four things someone needs to feel before they'll be persuaded into action." Never open a section with "It's four things, and they're called BENS" when nobody has said there's a system yet.

**A signpost fits where the viewer is in the lesson.** Pick the move for this point in the lesson's story, rather than because it's a strong line. A warning like "get this next part wrong and none of the rest matters" only works once there's a run of parts or steps, and before the system has been introduced it makes the viewer ask "what part?". Early in a lesson, signposts promise the result the whole lesson gets them, through client proof (B32), the pain it removes (B9) or the result it gets (B8), for example "So let's start by getting rid of the request that kills your best ideas, which is 'can you add a bit more detail', so the next one you take in comes back with a start date rather than homework." Warnings, rankings and "step three" labels come once the steps exist, and they still carry what the step gets them. Judge every signpost with the paragraph before it and the one after it, never on its own.

**Say the thing, never the feeling about it.** Lines that announce an emotion or label the next line ("here's the bit that stings", "this is the part that makes it unfair", "this is where it gets frustrating", "and this is what hurts") are abstract and sit outside their world. Say the concrete thing that causes the feeling and let them feel it. "Here's why your report gets ignored and theirs doesn't, even though yours had all the detail." "You had every number, they had two sentences, and the two sentences won."

**No "I said I'd show you".** Lines that point back to an earlier promise ("so I said I'd show you", "remember I said", "because I said you'd hear", "here's the one thing I said you could control") only work when the viewer has been waiting on a genuine gap, and they kept ending up on routine points, so they're out. When a loop gets paid off, just give them the thing.

**Pain and result are concrete, in their world.** Every pain and every result is something that happens, something someone says, or something that turns up in their inbox. "The executive replies the same afternoon with 'let's do it'" is a result. "Gets the yes" is a hint at one. Part C lists the vague words and what to use instead.

**Never the same wording twice in a lesson.** When a section's pain is being told to wait, rotate through the K1 wordings, and the same goes for every frustration and every result. Repeating a phrase ("let's revisit after the restructure") three times in a lesson is the clearest sign a script was written from memory rather than from the bank.

**Signposts pull with the result, the stakes or a curiosity gap, and it's there in the line or the very next one.** Any move from B1 to B12 works, and so do B32 and a Parker line. Some carry "so you can". Many don't need to, because a warning, a ranking word, client proof or the problem the last section left behind does the pulling on its own. Use "so you can" or "so you don't" in no more than one signpost in three, and never twice in a row.

**What never goes in a signpost.** The mechanism of the next section, the presenter as the subject, anything meta about the problem ("they're the same problem", "it's one cause"), understanding or insight as the prize ("once you can see it", "the part that makes this stick", "because that's where most recommendations stall"), where, why or how something goes wrong as the reason to keep watching, a bare question with no promise attached, a bare move into an example, and anything the viewer has to decode before they feel it.

**The hook is short.** It opens on a curiosity line, a question or a bold statement, gives one quick line of authority, then goes straight into "I'll show you" one to three things, at least one of them a Parker line, in about fifty to seventy words (see The hook below). The relatable scene and any build-up belong in the first section.

**No call to action straight after the hook.** Nothing asks the viewer to download, subscribe or click until they've had the first real piece of value. A lead magnet mention straight after the hook kills the pace, because they're being asked for something before they've been given anything.

**The turn into the mechanics is a Parker line.** Wherever the video moves from the idea to the tangible thing it shows (the slide, the line, the steps, the question), the "let me show you" that leads in promises that tangible thing, the result it gets and the objection it gets round (see Parker lines below).

**One story at most, and its first line is the pain.** A story is encouraged and never required, and a script never has more than one. It's never announced, so the signpost grounds the viewer and a connective like "Right, so" takes them straight in. Its first line hits the pain, and the scene comes after. It's told to the viewer with the thought behind every beat, runs through the eight beats in Stories below, keeps the mechanism back, and hands off to the teaching on a curiosity gap. The rest of the script calls back to it.

**Client signals stay subtle.** Two or three times a script, a signpost or a keep-watching line swaps the viewer for Josh's clients, or promises the next section as what his clients have been using. Nothing more is ever said about the offer.

**Lead magnet mentions sell the result.** When there's a lead magnet, it's mentioned three times, the first only once the viewer has had the first real piece of value, then around the middle and around 80 per cent, each time with a clear call to get it free from the description. Each mention is a value promise, the way a signpost is, and never a list of what's inside it.

**The close never sounds like an ending.** It comes straight after the core lesson with no summary and no wrap-up language, opens a new curiosity gap, and sends them to the next video as the answer (B31).

**Neutral lines get a pain or a result.** Any sentence that describes how something works in neutral terms is a chance to put the viewer's week back in front of them. "And that's what makes it effective" becomes "and that's what means you end this cycle with a decision rather than another date in the diary".

## What an Ed-level section sounds like

Here's a section from the black box lesson, written this way.

> But knowing what happens in the box doesn't help much when you're sitting there and they say "can you add some detail", and you can't tell whether to cut it down or pile more in. So this next part settles that.
>
> There are two things people tell me all the time. They get told it's too much detail and then asked for more, and they get talked round in circles. And both of them come straight out of the box.
>
> Take the detail first, because it's the one that makes you feel like you're going mad. You give the full version and you're told you lost them. You give the short version and you're asked for more. So which one do they want? Well, neither. They wanted the version that answered the question they were listening for, which is what does this mean for the thing I'm on the hook for.
>
> Because "more detail" from someone senior very often means "I'm not convinced." They won't say "I don't believe this," because they'd have to explain why, so "more detail" is the polite version, and it costs them nothing.
>
> The thing is, that's one meeting. Do it every week and it's the quarter where nothing you pushed for moved. Do it every quarter and it's the year they told you you'd be top of the list, and then the one after.

The moves in it, in order, are the problem the last section left behind (B3), grounding (B13), a quick question with a short answer (B19), the real meaning of "more detail" said plainly, and a stakes ladder with a career pain on the top rung (B25, K2). The idea itself is one sentence. Everything else is movement.

## A first signpost that works

Here's the signpost that takes a lesson from its opening scene into the teaching, written as client proof with a twist (B32) and handed off on a Parker line.

> Every client I've worked with has been through this. But once they stopped bringing more detail and started bringing the four things executives are actually waiting to hear, which I'll show you in a minute, their ideas started getting approved. They stopped being asked for more detail and started being asked for their opinion, and before long they were being talked about as leadership material. And the crazy thing is, the ideas were exactly the same.
>
> So let me show you what those four things are, so you can turn forty-five slides of great ideas that go nowhere into changes that happen, no matter how technical your work is.

It promises a result the viewer wants, chains it up to the career result they want most, opens a gap (what are the four things?), and the twist tells them the gap is the only thing standing between them and that result.

## Signposts that were sent back, and why

- "So where do you start when you've got an idea you know would help the business, and every time you take it to the executive you're sent away for more detail?" Neutral. It asks where to begin and promises nothing about what's coming or what they'll get.
- "So let's take one idea and follow it all the way through." A bare move into an example, with no reason for the viewer to care. Client proof with a twist (see A first signpost that works) does the job.
- "So let's start with what happens after you've explained it, because that's where most recommendations stall." Insight as the prize. The reason to keep watching is learning where things stall, with no pain or result in their world.
- "Now here's the part that makes this stick, so you catch the weak version of your own line before it's in front of someone senior." Abstract. Making something stick and catching the weak version are both a step or two away from anything the viewer wants.
- "So why does an idea everyone agrees with still go nowhere? Let me show you, so the next one you pitch moves." "Moves" hints at a result without naming one.
- "…so you can be the one who says three sentences and gets the yes." "Gets the yes" is vague.
- "…because they're the same problem in two different outfits, and once you see that you'll stop fixing the wrong one." Meta about the problem.
- "…because that gap is where every 'add some detail' comes from, and once you can see it…" A reason and an insight, with no result.
- "and handled right, a no is the moment they see you at your best." One step away from the result. "So you walk out with a next step and a date" is the result.
- "Let me show you the day I found out what I'd been getting wrong." About the presenter.
- "It's one sentence and it's the same shape every time." Shape is not a result.
- "And here's the bit that stings." "This is the bit that makes it feel unfair." Both announce a feeling instead of saying the thing that causes it.
- Five signposts in a row that each end "so you can…". Every one passes on its own and the script still sounds like a template.
- "Get this next part wrong and none of the rest matters…" as the lead-in to the first mention of the system. Strong on its own and wrong in that spot, because there were no parts yet.
- "It's four things, and they're called BENS." as a section opener, when the viewer hadn't been told a system was coming. Announce it first.
- "So now go back to the meeting from the start, because I said your colleague got thanked, and here's why." It announces a payoff for a routine point. "So let's go back to that meeting" does the job.
- "So let's start with getting your boss to take your recommendation into the meeting where it can be approved." The viewer isn't the one on the line. The video is about their idea getting rejected, so the signpost is about their idea.

## The other pieces of a script

**Parker lines.** These are named after Parker Worth's "inside you'll discover" bullets, and they're how a script promises something the viewer can picture having. A Parker line hints at a specific, tangible thing, says the result it gets them, and answers the objection they're already thinking in a closing clause. "The one phrase you can use to get any executive to back your idea, even if they've never worked with you before."

*How one is built.*

1. **The tangible thing, with a count where it's true.** The 3 things, the 1 rule, the 2 lines, the 4 slides, the one question, the exact words. Something the viewer could picture doing or holding. Never how it works, so "the phrase that gives executives a choice instead of a yes or no" fails, because it explains the method and makes the mechanism the prize. The count has to match what the video actually shows, and names of ideas taught in the video (the black box, BENS, SCR) stay out, because the viewer only understands them after the lesson.
2. **The result, in their world.** What happens because of it, taken from Part A. The decision in the meeting, the deck done in an afternoon, the executive seeing it the first time, your name staying on it. Being first, leading with something or putting something at the top is a step, and nobody wants a step.
3. **The objection clause.** On a sales page it sits in brackets. In a script it's spoken, so it becomes a clause after a comma, starting "even if", "even when", "no matter how" or "without". Take it from the bracket bank below, or from the doubt the viewer has at that exact point. Leave it off when no objection fits rather than forcing one.

*Where they go.*

- **The hook.** At least one of the things in "I'll show you" is a Parker line, and usually it's the one that names the tangible thing the video shows.
- **The turn into the mechanics.** Every "let me show you" where the video moves from the idea to the tangible thing is a Parker line, because the next section is where Josh shows the thing that gets the benefit without the objection. "So let me show you the 2 lines at the top of a slide that get an executive to see what you're asking for before they've read anything else, even if the rest of the slide is pure detail."
- **How many.** Usually two to four in a script, the hook plus one at each turn into the mechanics. They count as that signpost's move, so the signpost rules still apply, never two in a row, and never two worded the same way. When the hook and the turn promise the same thing, make the second one more specific than the first.

**Parker lines in our voice.** Adapt these or use them as patterns. `claude/curiosity-lines.md` in the project has the full set, and Parker's originals are in `09_Email/Swipe-Files/parker-checkout-and-bumps.md`.

- the one change to how you explain your work that gets executives to see it the first time, even if your work is too technical to fit in a sentence
- the 4 slides that get executives to approve your idea, so you build the deck in an hour instead of a weekend
- the one question that steers a meeting going round in circles back to a decision
- the exact words for every tough question, so you answer once and never sound defensive, even when you don't have the number to hand
- the one sentence that keeps your name on your work when it's repeated two levels up without you
- the 2 closing lines that end every presentation with a clear next step, whether they say yes or no
- the simple test that cuts a 40-slide deck down to the few that matter, without losing anything they might ask for
- the 3 lines that let you raise a problem with a senior person's idea without becoming the problem
- the 4 things that turn forty-five slides of great ideas that go nowhere into changes that happen, no matter how technical your work is

*Parker lines that were sent back, and why.*

- "How to get your ideas approved." There's no tangible thing in it, so there's nothing to picture.
- "The phrase that gives executives a choice instead of a yes or no." It explains the method.
- "The 7 steps to a deck that gets signed off", in a video that shows three. The count has to match.
- "The SCR framework that gets you a decision." It names an idea the viewer hasn't been taught yet.
- "The opening line that puts the answer first." It ends on a step, with no result.

**The bracket bank.** Objections the viewer is already thinking, to go at the end of a line.

- even if English isn't your first language
- even when your executives are the hard-to-read kind
- without getting louder, performing or playing politics
- even if your company makes you use its own template
- even when it already had everything in it
- even if you're not the most senior person in the meeting
- without it sounding rehearsed
- even if you only find out about the meeting that morning
- without rebuilding the deck
- even if your work is too technical to fit in three sentences
- no matter how technical your work is
- even if your boss is the problem
- without sounding like you're selling
- even if you've done presentation training and it didn't stick
- even if you've been told to "be more concise" with nothing attached

**The hook.** The hook is short, because the viewer decides whether to stay in the first few seconds. It has three beats and nothing else.

1. **The opener.** One line that makes them want the rest, either a curiosity line, a question with their pain in it, or a bold statement. "Why do executives just not get your work, even when you've explained every single detail?" Nothing comes before it, so there's no scene setting and no "have you ever wondered".
2. **Quick authority.** One short line that shows why Josh is the expert on this exact subject. The scale is more than 80,000 professionals taught, so the line runs "After teaching more than 80,000 professionals how to X, I …", with X fitted to the video and the end of the line pointing at the video's idea. Never mention reviews, star ratings or review counts, because they don't interest the viewer. It has to be true, so any other number or claim goes in square brackets for him to check. Having lived through the pain makes him relatable, which is a different job, so a line like "I've built more decks that went nowhere than anyone" doesn't count.
3. **I'll show you.** "So in this video I'll show you" one to three things the viewer wants, in their world. At least one is a Parker line, naming the tangible thing, the result and the objection. Never describe what happens in the video ("you'll watch a slide get rebuilt"), and never name the mechanism. When there's more than one, build each one differently, so one can end on the result, one on the pain it removes and one on a stake. They pay off the title and the thumbnail, and each is short enough to say in a breath.

The whole hook comes in at about fifty to seventy words, under twenty-five seconds on camera. The relatable scene ("you spent three days on the analysis…") and any build-up go in the first section, which starts straight after the hook. No call to action comes next, because they haven't had anything yet.

Here's one written that way, for a video on being told you're top of the list.

> You've been told you're top of the list for promotion two years running, and both years someone else got the job.
>
> After teaching more than 80,000 professionals how to get promoted, I know exactly how that decision gets made.
>
> So in this video I'll show you who really decides your promotion, and the one sentence that turns "next year" into a start date, even if your boss has already said there's no budget.

*Hooks that were sent back, and why.*

- A five-beat hook of 140 words, with a relatable scene and a hint at the core before the promise. The viewer had to wait too long for the reason to stay.
- A lead magnet mention straight after the hook. They're asked for something before they've been given anything, and the pace drops just as it got going.
- "I run a data and reporting team, so I've built more information that went nowhere than most people ever will." It shows he's lived the pain, and says nothing about why he's the expert on fixing it.
- An authority line ending "with over 9,500 five-star reviews". Nobody watching cares about the review count, and it slows the hook down.
- "You'll watch a boring slide rebuilt until the questions stop." Watching something happen in the video isn't something the viewer wants. "The simple framework that turns your most boring slides into decisions" is.
- "You'll get the four things that turn an update into a decision." It leads with the mechanism, when the promise should lead with what they walk away with.
- An "I'll show you" with no Parker line in it. Each thing can pass on its own and the hook still gives the viewer nothing tangible to picture.
- "I'll show you the four lines that get your boss to take your idea to the people who approve it." The viewer is the one whose idea is on the line, so the promise is their idea approved, never their boss carrying it.

**Stories.** Not every script needs a story, though one is encouraged wherever it fits, and a script never has more than one.

*Where it goes.* A story can sit in one of two places.

1. Straight after the first segment, once that segment has set up the key lesson of the video. Here the story is Josh's own run-in with the problem the lesson solves.
2. Later in the script, at the point where the viewer holds a belief that would stop them acting on the lesson. The set-up names the belief and waves it away with the value they'll get, in the shape "and if you're thinking / worried / concerned that X, then don't be, because you're going to get Y". "And if you're worried that cutting forty slides to four means getting caught out when they ask for the detail, don't be, because you'll walk out with more of their questions answered than you've ever had." Then the story goes straight in, as the tool that overturns the belief.

*Getting into it.* Never announce a story. No "let me tell you a story", "here's what happened to me" or "I'll never forget the day". The signpost before it grounds the viewer in what they're about to learn, and then the story starts on a connective like "Right, so" or "So a few years ago" and goes straight into the pain.

*Telling it.* The eight beats below are the skeleton, and the telling is Josh talking to the viewer across a table. Paint the picture with small concrete details they can see, like the look on the VP's face, the file name or the Google search at one in the morning, and keep handing the feeling back to them ("you know when you know, right?"). Every beat carries the thought behind it as well as what happened, so the viewer hears why he did what he did. If a draft reads like eight labelled steps with one line each, it's too stiff, so go back and let each beat breathe.

*The eight beats.*

1. **Hit the pain.** The first line is the pain itself, the consequence that was on the line, stated flat out. "I nearly got fired because of one VP." "I almost lost the biggest project of my career because I couldn't explain it." Then unpack it a little, what kept happening and what it looked like, so the viewer recognises it from their own week, and only then drop into the scene, the eleven o'clock, the file name, the meeting in the morning. The scene is the way into the pain, so a story that opens on "there I was at eleven at night on slide fifty-six" has the pain arriving too late.
2. **The wrong way, and the thought behind it.** What Josh did, which is what the viewer would do too, and the reason it felt right at the time, meaning what he hoped would happen or what he was scared of. "I added more slides, because he kept catching me on things I hadn't covered, and I thought if I could get every answer into the deck, he'd finally run out of questions."
3. **Why it fails, and the bind he's in.** Show why the wrong way won't work, and the trap of knowing it's wrong without knowing what else to do. "I knew forty-six slides was too many, but I couldn't work out what to cut, because it all felt important, and what if he asked about the one I'd deleted and I got caught out all over again?" This raises the stakes and adds urgency.
4. **The turn comes from somewhere.** The realisation grows out of something he noticed while he was doing the wrong thing, and then turns into "what if I did X instead, then he wouldn't Y". "But as I was adding detail to slide fourteen for the fifth time, I noticed that every time I'd put more detail in front of him, that detail was exactly what he'd asked about. So what if I did X instead?" Hint at the solution and hold the mechanism back. Never just "then it clicked".
5. **"So that's what I did", then dial up the doubt.** He acts on it, still without the mechanics. Then give the doubt room, with the specific worries in his head and what he did because of them. "I'd cut too much. Maybe this was a mistake. I was googling how to restore an old version of the deck to get it all back."
6. **The result, with the reason.** Don't jump straight to the outcome. Say that what he feared didn't happen, why it didn't, and what that meant, and then the outcome. "But that didn't happen, because once he saw the slides, he got what he'd been after, which meant there was nothing left to question, and I wasn't getting drilled." Still not the whole mechanism.
7. **The gap.** Some viewers will now feel they've got it and can do it themselves. So show them the piece that's missing between the idea and doing it. "Now, before you go and start cutting slides, you need to know which ones to cut, because without a system you'll cut the slide that was the answer unless you know the difference." Avoid "you might be thinking that's easy", which argues with the viewer instead of opening the gap.
8. **The handoff.** The story ends and leads into the explanation, whether that's the three steps, the framework he used, or whatever the teaching is. This handoff is a turn into the mechanics, so it's a Parker line. "So let me give you the three steps I use now to find the slides that matter, so you know what stays before you've even opened the file."

Here's one written that way, for the first placement, starting from the signpost at the end of the first segment.

> So what do you do when you've explained it every way you know and they still don't get it?
>
> Right, so a few years ago I nearly lost the biggest project of my career, because I couldn't explain it to one VP. I'd pitched it to him five times, and five times he shot it down. Every time I started explaining, he'd cut in with a question, I'd answer it, and he'd find another one, and after a while I could see it on his face, that look that says "who is this guy?"
>
> And the sixth meeting was at nine the next morning. So there I am at eleven at night on a file called final_final_v2, knowing that if this one goes like the last five, the project's gone and my name goes with it.
>
> So what did I do? I added slides. Every time I'd been in front of him, he'd caught me on something I hadn't covered, and I thought if I could just get every answer into the deck, he'd run out of questions and I'd finally convince him. So I built a backup slide for every question he'd asked before and one for every question I thought he might ask, and by midnight the deck was forty-six slides long.
>
> And I knew forty-six was too many. You know when you know, right? But I couldn't work out what to cut, because all of it felt important, and every time I went to delete a slide I'd think, what if that's the one he asks about? What if I get caught out again, in front of my boss, for the sixth time?
>
> But as I was putting more detail into slide fourteen for about the fifth time that night, I noticed something. Every time I'd put more detail in front of him, that detail was exactly what he'd asked about. He'd been questioning me on the very things I kept giving him. So what if I only showed him the answer to the one question he was really asking, and gave him nothing else to pick at?
>
> So that's what I did. I cut it from forty-six slides down to four. And then I sat and stared at those four slides and felt sick, because I'd just deleted weeks of work the night before the most important meeting I'd had in years. What if I'd cut the wrong thing? What if he asked about slide thirty and I had nothing? At about one in the morning I was googling how to restore an old version of a PowerPoint file, because I was sure I'd just made the biggest mistake of the whole project.
>
> But the grilling never came. He looked at the first slide and got what he'd been digging for through five meetings of my detail, and because it was right there in front of him, there was nothing left to question, so there was no drilling, no "can you go back to that one", and no look across the table. He asked one thing, which was when we could start, and I was out in twelve minutes with a yes.
>
> Now, before you go and start cutting slides out of your next deck, those four only worked because they were the right four. Cut without knowing which slide holds the answer they're walking in for, and you'll delete that one, keep the ones they don't care about, and walk in with less than you had and the same questions coming at you.
>
> So let me give you the three steps I use now to find the slides that matter, so you know what stays before you've even opened the file.

*Stories that were sent back, and why.*

- "And I know that one well, because at quarter to eleven on a Wednesday I was on a file called final_final_v2." It opens on the scene, so the pain arrives too late.
- "So I did what I always did, which was add slides." It names the wrong way without the thought that made it feel right.
- "And then, sitting there at quarter past twelve, it clicked." The turn comes from nowhere, when it should grow out of something he noticed.
- "…and then I lay awake convinced I'd just deleted every answer I was going to need." One line of doubt is too thin to raise any tension.
- "He asked one question, and it was when we could start." Straight after the doubt, this is too abrupt, because the viewer never hears why the fear didn't come true.
- "Now, you might be thinking that's easy, cut the deck and lead with the answer." It argues with the viewer instead of showing them the piece they're missing.
- A first draft that hits all eight beats in one or two lines each. Every beat is there and the story still sounds like a form being filled in.

*Calling back to it.* Once the story's been told, the rest of the script keeps tying its points back to what happened. "Which is why that VP ran out of questions." "And that explains why I was out in twelve minutes." "And that's what I learned at one in the morning, googling how to get my deck back." Aim for at least two callbacks after the story, each in a different shape, and treat the story as the running example (B26).

Never put a lead magnet mention inside a story. If the first mention would fall in the middle of one, it goes straight after it.

**Lead magnets.** These only go in once Josh has confirmed the video has one and told you what it does and what's inside it (see "Before pass one"). The magnet is mentioned three times, the first only once the viewer has had the first real piece of value, then around 50 and around 80 per cent of the way through, and every mention ends with a clear call to action, that it's free in the description or they can download it now, worded differently each time.

Every mention sells the result, the way a signpost does, and never the mechanics or features. "Twenty-seven templates across six categories" is a feature. "You'll have your first sentence ready before you walk into your next meeting" is the value.

1. **After the first piece of value, the shortest.** Never straight after the hook, because nothing's been given yet and a download request there kills the pace just as it got going. It goes once the first real idea has been delivered, usually somewhere between 25 and 35 per cent. Its only job is to make them aware the magnet exists and what it gets them. Set up a quick problem or challenge with the overall strategy, say the magnet solves it, give the call to action, and get back into the script.
2. **Around 50 per cent, after a core reveal.** It comes straight after one of the video's big insights. The signpost is a curiosity gap that they'll find this hard to do for themselves, and the magnet does it for them.
3. **Around 80 per cent, after the main lesson.** Once the big benefit of the video has been delivered, the magnet comes back as a value add. Getting that result for themselves, without the work, is what the magnet delivers.

Here are the three for a toolkit of opening lines. They're patterns to follow, so build the real ones from what Josh says his magnet does, never from these.

> After the first piece of value. The hardest part of all this is the first sentence, because it changes with every person you're in front of. So I've put the openers I use into a free toolkit, and it's in the description, so grab it now and you'll have one ready for your next meeting.
>
> Around 50 per cent. Now that you know what they're listening for, you'd think the line writes itself. The first few times, it won't, and you'll end up saying the background again, only shorter. The toolkit takes you from what you know to the exact sentence you'll say, and you can download it free from the description right now.
>
> Around 80 per cent. So you can now walk into a meeting and walk out with a yes, an owner and a date. And if you want that for every meeting in your diary without building the line from scratch each time, that's what the toolkit gives you. It's free in the description, so go and grab it.

**Client signals.** Every video signals, subtly and sparingly, that Josh works with clients, so the viewer picks up that there's an offer without ever being sold it. Nothing more is said about it, so never name the programme, a price, a call to book or a link. Two or three signals in a script is plenty, spread out, never two in back-to-back sections, and never in the same paragraph as a lead magnet call to action. They come in three forms.

1. **Swapping the viewer for the client.** Anywhere there's a signpost or a line that keeps them watching, the viewer can be swapped for Josh's clients, as long as the line still carries the pain or the result.
   - This is the part the people I work with get stuck on first.
   - If you're like most of my clients, this is exactly where the "can you add some detail" comes from.
   - Every client I've worked with has had a version of this meeting.
   - So stay with me, because this is the bit my clients end up using in every meeting they walk into.
2. **A value promise through the clients.** The next section is promised as what's been working for his clients.
   - So let me show you the framework my clients have been using to get results like this. [client result on screen]
   - This is where my clients have been getting their biggest wins.
   - Here's what my clients did to go from "top of the list" to an offer letter.
3. **Client proof with a twist (B32).** What his clients had, what changed once they started doing what the video teaches, the results that followed, and the twist, handed off on a Parker line. It's the strongest first signpost there is.

A general line about what clients struggle with or what changed for them is fine anywhere it fits. A specific result has to be real, so take it from `02_Testimonials` or ask Josh, and if you can't confirm one, leave a marked placeholder like [client result, Josh to confirm] rather than inventing a win. When a pain comes from a real client call, keep the client unnamed and general enough that they wouldn't be recognised.

**Explaining a concept.** Their situation first, the concept second, and keep it short. "Every conversation you have at work is a negotiation, whether you treat it as one or not. Which means every conversation that ends with 'there's no budget this year' or 'you're on the list for next time' is you accepting your career on their terms."

**Frameworks and phrase banks.** State the formula once, then give the phrases. Don't restate the formula under each phrase, and don't handle objections one by one when the handling is the same.

**The close.** It comes at the very end and points them to the next video with "so go and watch this video next". At no point should the viewer feel the video is ending. Once they've had the core value, nothing summarises and nothing sounds like wrapping up. The close follows straight on from the core lesson as a continuation, and it still has to leave them feeling they got a whole video rather than half of one. It does both by opening an entirely new curiosity gap, in one of two ways.

1. **Something that could undercut the whole video.** Everything they've just learned works best when there's something in place they don't have yet, so if they want the result, they'll need that first.
2. **A new problem.** The result they've just been given opens up a problem they didn't have before.

Either way, the next video is the answer, and the send carries the value they'll get or the problem it saves them from, as in "so go and watch this video next, where I'll show you…". In a course lesson the send points to the next lesson in the same way.

Four short beats at most. A line that opens the gap, one or two lines on it, the thing they still need, and the send with the result attached. Here's the first way.

> Now, there's one catch with all of this, and it has nothing to do with what you say in the conversation.
>
> You can run it perfectly, get every agreement and walk out with a next step written down, and then watch nothing happen for six weeks, because the person you had it with was never the one who decides.
>
> Which means the people above them need to want you promoted before you ever sit down.
>
> So go and watch this video next, and I'll show you who needs to be behind you and how to get them there, so the next time you ask, your boss has already been told to say yes.

And the second way, a new problem.

> The trouble with a yes in the meeting is what happens to it on Friday.
>
> Your boss takes it into the leadership meeting, says it in two sentences, and by the time it reaches the board it's their idea, with your name nowhere on it.
>
> Which means the work needs your name attached before it ever leaves the meeting.
>
> So go and watch this video next, and I'll show you how to do that, so when the board hears about it, they hear it was you.

There's also the doubt version of the first way, which starts "Now you might be thinking this won't work with your manager, and some of the time you'll be right…" and turns it into more promotions over a career.

Nothing between the core lesson and the close looks back over the video. Never use "so that's the whole thing", "to wrap up", "before you go", "that's it for today", "thanks for watching", "I hope this helped", "that's the next video", a list or summary of what was covered, "there's one thing I didn't tell you", or "all of that works inside the conversation, but".

Beyond it, the sales page in `10_Sales/Templates`, the call transcripts in `10_Sales/Calls` and the Calendly call recaps, and `08_Strategy/audience-communication.md` have the audience's own words. Every one of Ed's moves across twelve scripts is in `08_Strategy/2026-09_ed-lawrence_movement-library_v01.md` (project doc `claude/ed-lawrence-movement-library.md`), for when a section needs a move the bank doesn't cover. Parker lines come from `claude/curiosity-lines.md` in the project and `09_Email/Swipe-Files`.

## House style

These apply to every line, including quoted lines meant for the viewer to say.

- No em dashes anywhere. Restructure the sentence.
- Never use quiet, quietly, land, landed, honest or honestly, and never call a meeting "the room". The vague words in Part C go too.
- Say executive, senior leader or senior decision maker for the people they pitch to, never "the director" as the stand-in.
- No "it isn't X, it's Y" and no "you're not doing X, you're doing Y". Say what it is.
- No "Same X. Different Y." or "Same X. Same Y." fragment pairs.
- No signalling surprise. No "surprisingly", "more than you'd think", "contrary to what most people believe".
- No telling the viewer something is commonly missed or that most people don't realise it.
- No "here's why this matters". Say the thing that matters.
- No fragmented lists. "Nothing to trade. No way to force it. Pushing makes it worse." becomes one flowing sentence. Short spoken answers to a question ("Well, neither.") are fine, because that's speech.
- No colons splitting a sentence.
- Where one sentence is the consequence of the last, join them with a comma and a conjunction.
- British spelling, grade 12 reading level, a smart person talking.

## Before handing a script back

- Read it aloud in your head. Does it sound like Ed talking to one of our viewers? If a stretch sounds like a lesson being delivered, it needs another movement pass.
- Go through every signpost, including the first line after the hook and every line that moves into an example, and name its move from B1 to B12, B32 or a Parker line. Then ask what it promises the viewer. If you can't name the move, if it promises nothing (a bare question, a bare move into an example), if it promises where, why or how something goes wrong, or if it matches anything in "Signposts that were sent back", rewrite it before the script goes back.
- The viewer is the one whose idea, work or career is on the line from the hook to the close, and nothing turns the lesson into coaching someone else to carry it for them.
- The people they pitch to are executives, senior leaders or senior decision makers, never "the director".
- Every section has a signpost, a grounding line, and a closing line or handoff.
- No two signposts in a row use the same move, and "so you can" is in no more than one signpost in three.
- Inside every section, there's a move at least every four sentences.
- One running example is called back, any open loop is a genuine gap paid off by giving the thing, and nothing says "I said I'd show you" or "remember I said".
- No pain or result is worded the same way twice.
- Every pain and result is concrete, with nothing from Part C, and no line announces a feeling.
- The hook is an opener (a curiosity line, a question or a bold statement), one quick authority line using the 80,000 professionals with no mention of reviews, and "I'll show you" one to three things, at least one of them a Parker line, in about fifty to seventy words, paying off the thumbnail and title.
- Every turn from the idea into the tangible thing is led in by a Parker line, with a tangible thing whose count matches what the video shows, a result in their world, and an objection clause where one fits. No two Parker lines sit in a row or share wording.
- There's no more than one story. If there is one, it sits in one of its two places, is never announced, starts on a connective straight after a grounding signpost, and hits the pain in its first line before the scene. It runs the eight beats in order with the thought behind each one, the turn grows out of something noticed, the doubt gets room, the result says why the fear didn't come true, the mechanism stays back until after the handoff, it ends on the missing piece into the signpost, and it gets called back at least twice afterwards. Read it aloud, and if it sounds like beats being ticked off, it needs another pass.
- There are two or three client signals, spread out, each one a swapped signpost, a value promise or client proof with a twist, with nothing said about the offer and no invented client results.
- You asked Josh about a lead magnet before writing. If there is one, it's mentioned three times, the first only after the first real piece of value and never straight after the hook, then around 50 and 80 per cent. Each mention does its own job, sells the result with no features, ends on a clear call to get it free from the description, and none sits inside the story.
- The close starts straight after the core lesson, never summarises or sounds like an ending, opens a new curiosity gap, and sends them to "this video next" with the value or the problem it avoids attached.
- Every paragraph opens with a link to the one before it, and nothing is named before it's been introduced.
- Every signpost still makes sense read with the paragraphs either side of it.
- House style holds throughout.

# The movement bank

Lift lines straight from here, adapt them, or use them as the pattern for new ones. Ed's lines are from his twelve scripts with his dashes turned into commas.

## Part A · The frustration bank

Each frustration has the core in one line, then eight ways to say the pain, then six ways to say the result. Results are always a concrete event, something that happens, that someone says, or that turns up in their inbox.

### Communication frustrations

#### A1 · The work that gets ignored

**Core.** You put real work into something and it disappears without anyone acting on it.

Pain
- the deck you spent a week on gets skimmed in four minutes and never mentioned again
- they nod all the way through and then nothing happens
- your report goes out on Friday and the only reply is "thanks, useful"
- the update you sweated over gets "noted" and filed
- you watch the executive scroll past your slides to get to the next agenda item
- three weeks of analysis turns into one line in someone else's summary
- the paper sits in their inbox unopened until you chase it
- you present, they say "great work", and a month later nobody can remember what it said

Result
- the executive replies to your email the same afternoon with "let's do it"
- your report is the thing they quote at the next leadership meeting
- they stop you on slide three to ask when you can start
- your deck is still open on their screen when you leave
- the paper comes back with a decision written on it
- someone forwards your one-pager to their own boss with "we should do this"

#### A2 · Forty slides because you don't know which four matter

**Core.** Everything feels important, so nothing gets cut, and the important part drowns.

Pain
- you build forty slides because you can't work out which four they'll actually read
- the appendix is longer than the presentation
- you keep adding slides "just in case they ask"
- the one slide that matters is slide twenty-six
- you run out of time before you get to the point
- they flick to the end to find the answer and it isn't there
- you're still explaining the background when they check the clock
- the deck is thorough, accurate, and nobody gets to the bit that asks them for anything

Result
- you walk in with four slides and walk out with the budget
- they get the whole argument from the first slide
- the meeting finishes ten minutes early with a decision
- you know which slide they'll stop on before you've built it
- you spend the evening before the meeting at home rather than rebuilding the deck
- the senior leader says "that's all I needed" on slide three

#### A3 · Too much detail and no idea what to cut

**Core.** You know it's too long, you can't tell what's safe to lose, so it all stays in.

Pain
- you know it's too long and you still can't bring yourself to cut a line
- every paragraph feels like the one they'll ask about
- you cut a section, panic, and paste it back in
- your email needs a scroll bar
- you delete the detail and the whole thing suddenly sounds thin
- you can't tell the part that matters from the part you worked hardest on
- you rewrite the summary five times and it keeps getting longer
- someone says "can you get this to one page" and you can't see how

Result
- you know which line goes first and which goes in the appendix without agonising over it
- the email fits on one screen and still has everything in it
- you cut half the deck and it gets stronger
- the one-page version goes out and nobody asks for the long one
- you write the summary once
- the detail is all still there for whoever wants it, and nobody has to wade through it

#### A4 · "Cut it", then "add more"

**Core.** The feedback flips every time, so you can't win.

Pain
- you're told it's too much detail, so you cut it, and then you're asked for more
- you give them the short version and it's "a bit light", you give them the full one and it's "too much"
- every round of feedback contradicts the last
- you've lost count of the versions, and version seven looks a lot like version two
- you can't tell whether they want the headline or the workings
- you walk out of the review not knowing what they actually wanted changed
- you're rewriting for the third time and you still don't know what "better" looks like
- it feels like you're guessing at a moving target

Result
- the first version you send is the one that gets approved
- the feedback stops coming back as "more" or "less"
- you know exactly what they'll ask before they ask it
- the review is ten minutes and ends with "send it"
- your boss stops redrafting your work and starts forwarding it
- you stop keeping version numbers because there's only one

#### A5 · Sent away for more detail

**Core.** "Can you come back with more on this" is the polite way of saying no, and it keeps happening.

Pain
- you get sent away for more detail on something that already had all of it in
- "can you dig into this a bit more" and you know that's the end of it
- you come back with the extra detail and they still don't decide
- the proposal gets "let's revisit with more data" three meetings in a row
- you spend a week on the follow-up analysis and it gets two minutes on the agenda
- they ask for more and you can't tell what more is supposed to prove
- you've answered every question and they've still found another
- "more detail" has turned a two-week decision into a two-month one

Result
- they decide in the meeting, on what you brought
- the follow-up email is "approved" rather than a list of questions
- nobody asks for more because the answer to their real question was on page one
- the decision happens this week rather than next quarter
- you leave with a date rather than homework
- the extra analysis you would have done never needs doing

#### A6 · They don't see the work you did

**Core.** You did the hard part, and it comes across as something anyone could have done.

Pain
- your work sounds smaller when you describe it than when someone else does
- six months of effort comes out sounding like a task you ticked off
- they say "great, thanks" to something that saved them a fortune
- you describe what you did and hear yourself making it sound like admin
- they have no idea how hard it was, and you can't find a way to tell them without sounding like you're fishing
- the thing you're proudest of gets the same response as the monthly report
- by the time it reaches your boss's boss, it's half a sentence
- you did all the work and it looks like it just happened

Result
- the senior leader repeats your one line about it in their own meeting
- your name is on the slide when it goes to the board
- they ask you to present it to the leadership team yourself
- it comes up in your review before you've mentioned it
- your boss's boss knows exactly what you did without you having to tell them
- the work sounds as big when you say it as it was when you did it

#### A7 · Walking out without the action

**Core.** The meeting ends and nothing's been decided.

Pain
- you walk out with "let's pick this up next week" and no decision
- the meeting ends with twenty action items and none of them is the thing you needed
- the decision you needed gets pushed to the next steering meeting, and then the one after
- everyone agrees it's a good idea and nobody agrees to do it
- you leave with a follow-up meeting booked to discuss the follow-up
- they say "let's park this" and you know it's never coming back
- the minutes say "further discussion needed" again
- you've been raising the same item for a quarter and it's still on the agenda

Result
- you walk out with a yes, an owner and a date
- the budget is signed off before the meeting ends
- the next meeting on this is the one where you report the results
- the item comes off the agenda for good
- the executive says "go ahead" before you've finished the last slide
- the minutes have one line under your name, which is "approved"

#### A8 · Explaining what you've already explained

**Core.** You keep going over the same ground and it still doesn't seem to go in.

Pain
- you explain it, they ask, you explain it again, and they ask again
- it's the third meeting on this and you're still on the background
- they ask the question you answered on slide two
- you can see the moment they stop following and you don't know how to get them back
- you've said the same thing four ways and they still look lost
- they understood it last week and now you're starting from scratch
- you walk out thinking "I literally told them that"
- you're made to feel like you didn't have the whole picture when you were the one who did all the work

Result
- you explain it once and they repeat it back to you
- the question they were going to ask never gets asked
- they're talking about the next step before you've finished
- nobody asks you to "walk them through it again"
- the meeting you'd blocked an hour for takes fifteen minutes
- they get it in the first sentence and spend the rest of the time on what to do about it

#### A9 · Not knowing the words

**Core.** It's clear in your head and it falls apart the moment you start talking.

Pain
- it's perfectly clear in your head and it's gone the second you open your mouth
- you know what you think and you can't find the first sentence
- you hear yourself rambling and can't find the way out
- you think of exactly the right thing to say on the drive home
- you rehearse it in the shower and it comes out completely differently in the meeting
- you get to the end of your point and realise you never said what you wanted
- the words that get people moving seem to come easily to everyone else
- you open with the background because you don't know how else to start

Result
- you know your first sentence before you walk in
- you say it in three sentences and stop
- the words come out the way they sounded in your head
- you have the line ready for when the executive turns to you
- you finish your point and they're already nodding at the ask
- you stop replaying the meeting on the way home

#### A10 · Round in circles

**Core.** You can't tell what they want, and the conversation keeps looping.

Pain
- you go round in circles until you say "okay, whatever" and leave with nothing
- you ask a clear question and get a long answer to a different one
- every time you think you've agreed something, it gets reopened
- you're not sure what they're actually asking you for because it keeps changing
- twenty minutes in and you've drifted miles from the thing you came to ask
- they keep coming back to the one point you thought was settled
- you leave a one-to-one more confused about what they want than when you went in
- the same conversation happens every week with a slightly different ending

Result
- you get to the point in the first two minutes and stay there

#### B14 · Closing a section, and progress markers

**What it does.** Tells them that section's done and what they've now got, so they move on with you.

Ed
- "So that's the basics of the problem, which is kind of only scratching the surface of the damage it might be doing."
- "So now you know why story matters, the foundations, and how to plan one."
- "All right, so now you know why you need to do these, here's the system to make your own."

Ours
- And that's what step one is all about.
- So that's the first sentence sorted, and there's one piece left, which is what you ask for.
- So now you've got the benefit and the reason it's new, and all that's left is making it feel safe.
- So that's what the box does to you. Now let's make it work for you.
- So that's the win. Now for the harder one.
- So now you know why the last three career conversations ended with a date, let's make the next one end with a decision.
- That's the first half of the deck done, and it's the half that decides whether they keep listening.

### Inside sections

#### B15 · "The thing is"

**What it does.** The most-used re-hook in the library. It complicates the point just as it's settling.

Ed
- "The thing is, that's just one video."
- "The thing is, I track all of my sales metrics from YouTube."
- "The thing is, once you've shot this, you then need to edit it."

Ours
- The thing is, that was one meeting. Do it every quarter and it's the year they told you you'd be top of the list.
- The thing is, what they wanted was a different first sentence.
- The thing is, the person who summed it up in two sentences just went first.
- The thing is, your boss probably does want to move you. They just can't picture you in the job yet.
- The thing is, the moment you fix that one, three new objections turn up.
- The thing is, "more detail" very often means "I'm not convinced".
- The thing is, you already know all of this. You just haven't been saying it first.

#### B16 · "But worse" and "it gets even worse"

**What it does.** Climbs the stakes one more rung.

Ed
- "But worse, if your videos start solving problems your hottest viewers don't care about anymore, they stop clicking altogether."
- "But it gets even worse."

Ours
- But worse, the next time you raise something, they're already expecting the long version.
- And it gets worse, because the person who does get the decision is usually the one who summarised your work.
- But here's the worse part. Every time it gets pushed, it gets easier for them to push it again.
- And then it gets even worse, because by the time it reaches the board, your name's not on it.
- Worse still, "thanks, useful" is what they'll remember you for when the promotion comes up.
- But it gets worse, because the feedback that follows is "work on your presence", which tells you nothing.

#### B17 · "Here's the thing", "check this out", "this is where it gets interesting"

**What it does.** Turns their head towards the next point.

Ed
- "But this is where it gets interesting."
- "So check this out."
- "But here's the thing."
- "Now do you want to know something crazy?"

Ours
- But this is where it gets interesting.
- So check this out.
- Now here's what caught me out for years.
- But here's the thing about being sent away for more detail.
- Now watch what happens when we flip it round.

#### B18 · "But the second thing that really matters"

**What it does.** Stops one point feeling like the whole story, so they keep listening for the next.

Ed
- "But the second thing that really matters is…"

Ours
- But the second thing that matters just as much is what you ask for.
- And there's a second half to this, which is what happens after the meeting.
- But that's only half of it. The other half is who they hear it from.
- That gets you the decision. The second thing gets you the credit for it.

#### B19 · Rhetorical question, short answer

**What it does.** Asks what they're thinking and answers in a couple of words, which keeps the pace up and sounds like a conversation.

Ed
- "Would I buy that? Well, no."
- "But do you have to build a spreadsheet like I did? Well, no."
- "But I guess the question is, should you turn them off too? Well, no. Maybe not. And I'll explain."
- "But how do you find out how long people have been watching? Well, it's actually really easy."

Ours
- So which one do they want? Well, neither.
- So what do you do with all that detail? You move it.
- Does this work on a boss who talks in circles? It works best on them.
- Does that come from the box too? It does.
- Did I invent anything? No. Every fact's still in there.
- So is it you? It was the order you said it in.
- Should you raise every objection out loud? Not at all. Most of them get answered inside the line.
- So how long does this take? About ten minutes, once you've done it twice.

#### B20 · "Right?"

**What it does.** Asks for a nod, which makes it a conversation rather than a lecture.

Ed
- "Which makes sense, right?"
- "That would be absolutely insane, right?"
- "It's crazy, right?"

Ours
- Which makes sense, right? They've got eleven other things on today.
- You'd never buy a phone that way, right?
- That's mad, right? Three months of work, and one line in someone else's slide.
- You've seen this happen, right?
- Which is a strange way to run a promotion process, right?

#### B21 · The small open loop

**What it does.** Parks a promise for a few minutes' time and pays it off. Use it only for a genuine gap the viewer wants closed, and pay it off by giving them the thing, never by announcing that you're paying it off.

Ed
- "And you're going to find out why in a minute."
- "Now at the end of the video I'm going to show you what this AI is."
- "And that brings us on to the AI I showed you at the very start."

Ours
- And there's one thing in that box you can control, which I'll get to in a minute.
- Keep that vendor in your head, because we're coming back to it.
- And by the end, you'll hear one update said two ways and know in ten seconds which one gets signed off.
- There's a reason the three-sentence version works on the most senior person too, and you'll see why in a second.
- Remember the vendor? Here's what you said the first time.

#### B22 · Misconception setup

**What it does.** Makes their current belief the thing that's about to be overturned.

Ed
- "Okay, so to start, we just need to look at the common misconception about video quality."
- "Now, this is what people don't understand about business or YouTube."
- "The truth is, we're all completely wrong, but also right at the same time."

Ours
- You've probably been told the fix is to be more concise. So you cut it down, and they asked for more.
- Everyone tells you good work speaks for itself. It doesn't say a word unless you give it one.
- One person tells you to lead with the data. Another tells you to lead with the story. The truth is, they're both half right.
- We're told the promotion goes to whoever's done the most. Look at who got the last three and tell me that's true.
- You'd think the answer to "more detail" is more detail.
- The usual advice is to prepare harder. Preparing harder is how you end up with forty slides.

#### B23 · Conditionals that keep everyone watching

**What it does.** Splits the audience on the spot and gives each group a reason to stay.

Ed
- "So if you're in a position where your ad revenue makes a considerable difference to your life, no, do not turn it off. But if you're in a position where you have a business like mine…"
- "If you can't visually show a transformation, don't panic, because this can still work."
- "Now don't panic if you're not in the top. Trust me. You can."

Ours
- If you're presenting to a board, this is your opening line. If it's a one-to-one with your boss, it's the first thing you say when you sit down.
- And if you don't have a big presentation coming up, don't worry, because every update email works the same way.
- If your boss is the type who wants every detail, this still works, the detail just comes second.
- Now don't panic if you're not managing anyone yet. This is how you get the job where you do.
- If you're three levels from the top, this gets you noticed. If you're one level from it, this gets you the seat.
- And if English isn't your first language, good, because this is about order, and order works in every language.

#### B24 · "And when you do that?"

**What it does.** Ties the move to the result straight away, in their world.

Ed
- "And when you do that? You won't turn viewers off."
- "And what happens when you do that?"
- "By the third video, boom, they're on your email list or they're booking a call."

Ours
- And when you do that? The meeting ends with a decision and a date.
- And what happens when you do that? The question they were about to ask never comes.
- Do that, and by slide two they've stopped checking their phone.
- And when you do that, your boss stops redrafting your emails and starts forwarding them.
- Get that right, and the budget's signed before you reach the last slide.
- And when you do that? They start asking you what to do rather than what the numbers are.

#### B25 · Stakes ladder

**What it does.** Makes the cost concrete, then climbs it, with a re-hook on each rung.

Ed
- "I've lost $260 in sales. But if that was 1.5%, I'm down $790. The thing is, that's just one video."

Ours
- That's one meeting pushed a week. The thing is, that's one meeting. Do it every quarter and it's the year they said you'd be top of the list, and then the one after.
- One "thanks, useful" doesn't matter. Twelve of them is the reputation you walk into the promotion panel with.
- It's one weekend on a deck. Then it's every month. Then it's the reason you're too busy to do the work that would get you promoted.
- It's one idea someone else got credit for. Then it's the next one. Then it's the person they picture in the bigger job, and it isn't you.
- A two-week delay on one decision. Then on every decision. Then you're known as the person whose projects stall.
- One idea that dies is one idea. Three of them and you're the person with plenty of ideas who doesn't quite get the business, and that's what gets said about you in the promotion round.

#### B26 · Callback

**What it does.** Brings back a number, story or image from earlier, so separate points feel like one idea.

Ed
- "which is, remember, the lower estimate for the amount of people that will click on the ads before it."
- "because of what I told you"
- "Instead, what I think most people should do is what I did when I was homeless and lived in Airbnbs."

Ours
- Which is, remember, the question they walked in with.
- Remember the vendor? This is the same trick.
- It's the black box again. Your proof goes in, and it comes out through the picture they already had.
- Go back to the head of HR for a second.
- This is the three-hires request from the last lesson, and watch what happens to it now.
- You met this in the four tests, and here's where it earns its keep.
- Which is why that VP ran out of questions.
- And that explains why I was out in twelve minutes with a yes.
- And that's what I learned at one in the morning, googling how to get my deck back.

#### B27 · Prove it in front of them

**What it does.** Demonstrates rather than claims, and asks them to notice the difference.

Ed
- "What I'm going to do is deliver the same information now in two different ways, and then you can make your own mind up."
- "Can you feel the difference between the two?"
- "So what you can see on screen right now is, I'm in the same environment, yet the vibe and quality looks so different, right?"

Ours
- Here's the first version. And here's the other one. Which one would you decide on?
- Listen to the two side by side.
- Which one would you sign?
- I'm going to say the same update two ways, and you'll know in ten seconds which one gets approved.
- Now read it back and see if you can hear where they'd stop listening.
- Did anything change except the order?

#### B28 · Put them in a scene

**What it does.** A "have you ever" or "picture this" that drops them into a moment they've lived.

Ed
- "So have you ever been invited to someone's house, and walked in and gone, 'Oh my gosh, this person lives in an absolute pigsty.'"
- "Well, have you ever really craved chocolate, gone to your secret stash, opened the drawer, and there's nothing left?"

Ours
- You know this meeting. You've got something to raise, and fifteen minutes later you're still in it.
- Have you ever sent an email you were proud of and got "thanks" back, and nothing else?
- Picture the executive on slide twelve, checking the time.
- Have you ever driven home from a meeting and thought of exactly what you should have said?
- Think about the last time someone said three sentences and walked out with the decision.
- You've had this conversation. Your boss says "you're top of the list", and you smile, and you know.

#### B29 · Mock voice

**What it does.** Says what someone's thinking, in their words, which is funny and recognisable.

Ed
- "Came here for apple, this fool's giving me bananas. I'm off."
- "What are you doing, you weirdo, just walking around talking to a camera."

Ours
- And the executive's sitting there going, "I don't need the history, is my date at risk or not?"
- The CFO reads "phased approach over three stages" and thinks, "so that's next year, then."
- Your boss is thinking, "great, another forty slides."
- And you're sitting there going, "but you asked me for detail."
- And somewhere in the back of the head of HR's mind is "so my team gets replaced by a chatbot, then."

#### B30 · Pattern interrupt

**What it does.** A dry aside or bit of self-deprecation, placed just after dense teaching.

Ed
- "Would I buy that? Well, no. I only had that because I was a homeless man who couldn't fit anything else in my bag."
- "Illusion ruined."
- "Chocolate talk's getting me hungry. Flipping rice cake."

Ours
- I got a D in English, so if I can do this, you're fine.
- I say this as someone who once built a sixty-slide deck for a fifteen-minute meeting.
- Which, looking back, was a very long way to say "no".
- Nobody in the history of dishwashers was sold one by the manual.
- I know. Riveting.
- Stick with me, this is the good bit.

**What it does.** Opens the next problem and sends them to the lesson that fixes it, with the result attached. No recap.

## Part C · Words that sound like results and aren't

These hint at an outcome without saying what happens. Swap every one for the concrete event.

- "gets the yes" becomes the budget signed in the meeting, "go ahead" in the reply, the promotion going into this round
- "gets the nod" becomes they approve it before you've finished, your boss says "do it"
- "your pitch moves" or "it moves forward" becomes it goes to the board next week with your name on it
- "lands well" or "resonates" becomes they quote it back to you, they forward it to their boss
- "upstairs" or "above you" becomes the leadership meeting, the executive, your boss's boss, the promotion panel
- "at the table" or "in the room" becomes in the meeting, in the one-to-one, in front of the CFO
- "gets traction" becomes someone's name goes next to it with a date
- "buy-in" becomes they say yes, they put budget against it, they give you the people
- "stakeholders" becomes the head of HR, the CFO, your boss
- "executive presence" (as a result) becomes they ask you what to do, they invite you to the meeting where it's decided
- "stand out" becomes a senior leader mentions you by name
- "be heard" becomes they act on what you said
- "make an impact" becomes the specific thing that changed on Tuesday
